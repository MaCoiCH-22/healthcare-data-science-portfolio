3.16%–3.36% of the 4,466 completed studies could have posted results within 365 days. The lower bound includes 141 confirmed studies. The upper bound adds all nine with indeterminate timing, including chronology anomalies.

Why timing matters

When a clinical study reaches primary completion, how visible are its results in the registry? Research teams can use the counts to identify cohorts where missing or uncertain dates merit a closer look.

How soon were results posted?

The registry confirmed posting within 365 days for 141 studies. Nine more had uncertain posting times, which the sensitivity range accounts for.

Methods and implementation
  1. ClinicalTrials.gov API v2
  2. Frozen source snapshot
  3. Normalized SQLite cohort
  4. Date bounds and aggregates
  5. Expandable results by condition

The cohort contains interventional studies returned by searches for asthma, breast cancer, and type 2 diabetes, with actual primary completion in 2018–2023. Records are deduplicated by NCT ID in the overall view. The timing denominator includes studies with a registry status of Completed. Dates supplied at month or year precision are treated as intervals. The sensitivity range counts none versus all of the indeterminate dates, including chronology anomalies.

Limitations

Condition searches can overlap. Later completion cohorts had less time to post results by the snapshot date, so the year-by-year percentages alone cannot establish a change in reporting behavior. The results reflect one registry snapshot and sponsor-reported information. The 365-day measure describes first public posting relative to primary completion. Legal reporting deadlines, submission dates, exceptions, and publications outside the registry require separate evidence.

What I’d check next

For a study-level review, inspect underlying records for missing results or uncertain dates. A subsequent analysis could distinguish submission from public posting and use historical registry releases to measure changes over time.
